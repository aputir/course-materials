# From Stardust to Software

**A 43-slide presentation for computer science students · about 35–45 minutes**

## Story spine

Matter learns to hold patterns. Chemistry becomes self-maintaining systems; life adds inheritance, sensing, and movement; humans extend memory and coordination through tools and institutions; electricity and computers extend calculation and communication; machine learning lets computers fit patterns from data. The ending returns responsibility to the makers: capability grows, but consequences depend on what people build and reward.

### Visual direction

Minimal hand-drawn scientific sketch, warm ivory paper, graphite and fine ink, selective muted teal and ochre, one main idea per wide 16:9 frame, generous negative space, no text on the art. Reuse the first six existing illustrations for slides 1–6, the tree-of-life image for slide 15, and the human-looking-at-stars image for slide 20. The remaining 35 slides have matching image files alongside this script.

## Slide-by-slide script

### 1. A beginning without a center

**Visual:** A bright seed and fine expansion lines crossing an open field. **Say:** “The universe did not explode into empty space; space itself expanded. As it expanded, it cooled—and that cooling made the next layers of structure possible.”

### 2. Matter assembles

**Visual:** A few quark-like points bound into a compact particle. **Say:** “In the hot early universe, energy became particles. As conditions changed, quarks bound into protons and neutrons—the raw ingredients from which atomic nuclei could form.”

### 3. The universe turns transparent

**Visual:** One clean atom with a few rays of light moving through open space. **Say:** “About 380,000 years later, electrons could stay bound to nuclei. Light stopped scattering constantly and traveled freely. The universe acquired both its first simple atoms and its oldest visible afterglow.”

### 4. Stars manufacture new ingredients

**Visual:** A cutaway star with a bright core and a few grains drifting outward. **Say:** “Gravity gathered gas into stars. Fusion built many of the heavier elements; stellar explosions and other extreme events spread more of them into space. The atoms in planets—and in us—have a cosmic history.”

### 5. A planet becomes a chemistry lab

**Visual:** A young sun, a thin disk, and a forming blue-green planet. **Say:** “About 4.5 billion years ago, the Sun and planets formed from enriched cosmic material. Earth cooled into a world with rock, water, heat, and chemical gradients—conditions that could keep reactions going.”

### 6. The unsolved transition

**Visual:** An early seafloor vent, a small protocell, and spare molecule shapes. **Say:** “We know life existed by at least 3.5 billion years ago, but no one has reconstructed the full path from chemistry to the first evolving systems. This is an active scientific problem, not a finished origin story.”

### 7. Where could the chemistry happen?

**Visual:** A simple geothermal pool beside a deep-sea vent, both feeding small chemical clusters. **Say:** “Researchers investigate several environments: hydrothermal vents, volcanic pools, shorelines, and mineral surfaces. Each offers different combinations of heat, wet–dry cycles, pores, and energy. No location has won the case.”

### 8. Ingredients are not yet life

**Visual:** A few distinct molecular clusters gathering from a sparse chemical landscape. **Say:** “Early Earth could make or receive organic building blocks—among them amino-acid, lipid, and nucleotide precursors. Making a molecule is only step one; life also needs organization, usable energy, and a way for useful patterns to persist.”

### 9. Concentration changes the odds

**Visual:** A shallow pool evaporating, leaving molecules more concentrated along a mineral edge. **Say:** “In a huge dilute ocean, useful molecules rarely meet. Pores, mineral surfaces, freezing, and wet–dry cycles can concentrate ingredients and encourage reactions. Think of the environment as part of the chemistry, not just its backdrop.”

### 10. A molecule that can carry a pattern

**Visual:** One folded, hand-drawn RNA-like strand making a loose copy of itself. **Say:** “RNA is a compelling candidate for an early information molecule because it can store sequence information and some RNA molecules can catalyze reactions. The RNA-world idea is powerful, but a complete, proven path from prebiotic chemistry to RNA replication remains missing.”

### 11. A boundary creates an inside

**Visual:** A fatty membrane bubble enclosing a few simple molecules. **Say:** “Fat-like molecules can spontaneously form membrane bubbles in water. A boundary keeps some ingredients near one another and creates an inside distinct from the outside—a crucial step toward a cell, though a bubble alone is not alive.”

### 12. Copying makes evolution possible

**Visual:** Three protocell bubbles with slightly different inner patterns; one gives rise to more bubbles. **Say:** “Once a system can make imperfect copies, some variants persist or reproduce more successfully than others. Selection can begin acting on chemical systems before anything resembles a modern organism. Heredity turns chemistry into cumulative change.”

### 13. Life needs a power supply

**Visual:** A membrane with more particles on one side and a tiny molecular rotor using the difference. **Say:** “Living systems maintain themselves far from chemical equilibrium. Gradients—differences in ions, acidity, or available fuel—can store usable energy. Modern cells exploit proton gradients; how the earliest systems captured energy is still debated.”

### 14. Movement starts without a motor

**Visual:** A tiny cell drifting in water, nearby particles jostling and a gentle current carrying it. **Say:** “The first motion need not have been swimming. Molecules jiggle; fluids flow; cells drift, collide, and spread. At this scale, Brownian motion and currents move matter whether or not a cell can steer.”

### 15. Evolution branches, it does not climb

**Visual:** The existing spare tree-of-life illustration. **Say:** “Evolution has no finish line. It branches as populations vary and environments filter what reproduces. Every living species is a present-day branch, not a rung below another species.”

### 16. Life changes the planet

**Visual:** A microbial mat beneath sunlight, with a thin blue atmosphere beginning to change. **Say:** “Some microbes evolved photosynthesis. Oxygen-producing photosynthesis eventually transformed oceans and atmosphere in the Great Oxidation Event, around 2.4 billion years ago. A biological innovation changed the planet—and created new opportunities and new stresses.”

### 17. A cell becomes a community

**Visual:** One small cell inside a larger cell, with a few clean mitochondrial shapes. **Say:** “Eukaryotic cells contain internal compartments. Mitochondria are understood as descendants of bacteria that entered a lasting partnership inside another cell. Cooperation between once-independent life forms helped unlock new complexity.”

### 18. Many cells, different jobs

**Visual:** A few cells joining into a simple multicellular form, some beginning to specialize. **Say:** “Multicellularity evolved more than once. Cells could stick together, communicate, and specialize. The payoff was coordination and larger bodies; the cost was dependence—many cells lost the ability to live independently.”

### 19. Sensing creates directed movement

**Visual:** A simple microbe orienting toward a nutrient gradient, with a small rotating filament. **Say:** “Sensing and movement are separate innovations. Chemotaxis lets microbes bias their movement along chemical gradients. Flagella are molecular machines that can drive active swimming, but we should not assume the first life already had a modern motor.”

### 20. One branch becomes human

**Visual:** The existing human figure looking toward a star field. **Say:** “Humans are one recent branch of a much older tree. Bipedal walking, hands able to manipulate objects, long childhoods, social learning, and language-like communication accumulated over time; no single trait explains us alone.”

### 21. A stone becomes a tool

**Visual:** A hand striking one stone against another, producing a sharp flake. **Say:** “A tool stores a solution outside the body. A stone edge extends a hand; skill can be copied; a useful technique can outlive its inventor. Toolmaking becomes a second inheritance system alongside genes.”

### 22. Fire changes the energy budget

**Visual:** A small hearth, a person, and food warming above it. **Say:** “Controlled fire brought warmth, protection, and cooked food. It also changed where humans could live and how they gathered. The important shift is not ‘fire made us human’; it is that culture let a discovery become shared infrastructure.”

### 23. Language lets groups share invisible things

**Visual:** Two people coordinating around a simple shared mark or gesture. **Say:** “Language origins are difficult to date because speech leaves no direct fossil. But symbolic communication lets people coordinate over absent objects, distant plans, rules, and stories—information that no single person has to rediscover.”

### 24. Farming stores food—and creates new problems

**Visual:** Hands tending a small crop beside gathered grain. **Say:** “Agriculture made food production more predictable in some places, supporting denser settlements. It also brought labor, disease exposure, inequality, and vulnerability to harvest failure. Progress often changes the problem rather than ending it.”

### 25. Writing becomes external memory

**Visual:** A clay tablet beside a simple granary ledger and city wall. **Say:** “Writing began in part as practical recordkeeping. Once information could persist outside a person’s memory, societies could coordinate taxes, trade, laws, contracts, and histories across larger distances and longer times.”

### 26. Cooperation scales; conflict scales too

**Visual:** A road connecting a marketplace and a distant city, with a shield resting nearby. **Say:** “Cities and states coordinated irrigation, trade, roads, and public works. They also concentrated power and organized warfare. Technology rarely has one social effect: the same logistics that move grain can move armies.”

### 27. Knowledge accumulates unevenly

**Visual:** A small chain of teachers, notebooks, and workshop tools passed between generations. **Say:** “Human progress is not a smooth upward line. Knowledge grows through teaching, institutions, craft, migration, and exchange—and can be lost through war, censorship, or collapse. The key resource is a community able to preserve and test ideas.”

### 28. Printing multiplies the copy

**Visual:** A hand-operated press making several identical pages from one carved plate. **Say:** “Printing lowered the cost of copying ideas. More readers could compare the same text, challenge claims, and build on earlier work. It did not make knowledge automatically true; it made knowledge easier to circulate and contest.”

### 29. Measurement turns questions into models

**Visual:** A scientist measuring a falling object with a simple instrument and plotted curve, no labels. **Say:** “Modern science made measurement, mathematics, replication, and public criticism central tools. Models compress observations into useful structure. A model is not the world—it is a tested simplification with assumptions and error bars.”

### 30. Steam scales mechanical work

**Visual:** A compact steam engine driving one clean gear and belt. **Say:** “Industrial machines turned concentrated energy into repeatable work. Factories increased output and changed labor, cities, and trade. They also consumed resources at a scale that reshaped landscapes and accelerated climate change.”

### 31. Electricity makes distance feel smaller

**Visual:** A telegraph key sending a thin line of light along one wire toward a distant receiver. **Say:** “Electricity became a controllable signal as well as a source of power. The telegraph made messages travel much faster than people or ships. Long-distance coordination began to operate on a new clock.”

### 32. A switch can become a decision

**Visual:** A simple open-or-closed relay circuit leading to a few logical paths. **Say:** “A switch has two useful states: on and off. Boolean logic lets us compose those states into decisions; relays and later transistors made the decisions physical. Computation begins when we can reliably build logic out of hardware.”

### 33. The computer makes calculation programmable

**Visual:** A sparse sketch of a large early computer cabinet beside a person. **Say:** “Early electronic computers were built for demanding calculations, including ballistics. The deeper breakthrough was programmability: one machine could execute different procedures. Software separated the instruction from the physical machine.”

### 34. War accelerates computation—and secrecy

**Visual:** A cipher wheel, a computer operator, and a closed message passing through the machine. **Say:** “Wartime codebreaking and weapons calculations pushed computing forward. The same capabilities support defense, intelligence, and surveillance. The history of computation is also a history of who can calculate, observe, and keep secrets.”

### 35. Networks turn computers into a system

**Visual:** Four simple machines connected by a few packet-like dots along branching lines. **Say:** “Packet switching breaks information into pieces that can travel across shared links and reassemble at the destination. ARPANET connected its first four host computers in 1969; networking grew through research, public standards, and many institutions.”

### 36. Social networks optimize connection

**Visual:** A few people represented by nodes exchanging small message marks across a network. **Say:** “Social platforms make publishing and finding people dramatically easier. Their ranking systems decide what becomes visible. A feed is not a neutral window: it is a product shaped by objectives, feedback, and business models.”

### 37. Persuasion becomes programmable

**Visual:** A phone screen suggested only through shapes: one large bright choice, a small hidden exit, and a person hesitating. **Say:** “Dark patterns make a desired choice easy and an unwanted choice hard; social engineering targets trust and attention. For a programmer, interface decisions are powerful. Measure not only clicks, but whether users understood and chose freely.”

### 38. Statistics turns examples into estimates

**Visual:** Scattered observations flowing into a simple curve with a few uncertain points. **Say:** “Statistics helps us infer patterns from noisy samples. A model is a function that maps inputs to estimates; assumptions, data quality, and evaluation determine whether those estimates travel beyond the examples we saw.”

### 39. Rules give way to learning from examples

**Visual:** A small rulebook on one side and examples shaping a simple decision boundary on the other. **Say:** “Expert systems encode human-written rules: transparent in some ways, brittle when cases change. Machine learning instead adjusts model parameters from examples. It can generalize, but it can also learn the dataset’s blind spots.”

### 40. GPUs make large learning systems practical

**Visual:** A small neural network drawn as layered points, beside a GPU chip and one game-like scene. **Say:** “Neural networks learn layered representations by adjusting weights. GPUs accelerate the parallel matrix operations underneath them. Graphics helped drive GPU development, and the same parallel hardware later helped deep learning scale.”

### 41. Attention connects context

**Visual:** A short line of abstract tokens with a few fine attention connections converging on one generated continuation. **Say:** “Transformers use attention to relate parts of a sequence. Large language models learn statistical structure from text and generate likely continuations. Their fluent output can be useful, but fluency is not proof of truth or understanding.”

### 42. Generative AI becomes a programming partner

**Visual:** A CS student sketching an app while a small machine offers code-shaped marks on paper. **Say:** “Generative models can draft code, tests, explanations, images, and prototypes. Vibe coding can make a first demo fast; the engineering work remains: define requirements, inspect behavior, test edge cases, protect data, and own the result.”

### 43. The AI startup reality check

**Visual:** One small company growing from a seed-like idea toward a working product, with customers and a finite fuel gauge. **Say:** “Seed, Series A, B, and C are funding stages, not revenue. Funding buys time and capacity; revenue comes from customers; profit remains after costs like compute, people, and sales. Anthropic said in February 2026 that it had raised $30 billion in Series G and had $14 billion in run-rate revenue—company-reported figures, not profit. The question for builders is what value earns that investment, and who bears the cost.”
